An applicant tracking system (ATS) is the software employers use to collect job applications, store candidate details and move people through hiring stages. When you upload a resume, the ATS **parses** it: it pulls out your name, contact details, job titles, companies, dates, education and skills, and saves them as searchable fields.

An "ATS resume" is simply a resume built so that this parsing goes right.

## What happens to your resume after you click Apply

1. **Upload and parse.** The system extracts text from your file and tries to map it to fields.
2. **Store.** Your profile joins a database of candidates for that job and often for future jobs.
3. **Screen.** Recruiters filter by knockout questions (location, notice period, work authorisation) and search by keywords such as a job title or tool.
4. **Rank or match.** Many newer systems also score how well your profile matches the job, increasingly with AI. See [how AI screens resumes](/articles/how-ai-screens-resumes/).
5. **Human review.** A recruiter opens the resumes that surfaced.

The key point: a person usually decides, but they only see what the software showed them.

## What breaks parsing

| Problem | What can go wrong |
| --- | --- |
| Two or three columns | Text from side-by-side columns gets merged line by line into nonsense |
| Tables and text boxes | Content inside them may be skipped |
| Contact details in the header or footer | Some parsers ignore those areas, so your phone and email go missing |
| Icons instead of words | A phone icon next to a number isn't read as "Phone" |
| Images, charts, skill bars | Can't be read at all |
| Unusual headings like "My Journey" | The system may not recognise it as Experience |
| Scanned or image PDFs | No text exists to extract |
| Inconsistent dates | Experience length can be miscalculated |

## What an ATS-friendly resume looks like

- One column, top to bottom
- Standard headings: Summary, Experience, Education, Skills, Projects, Certifications
- Contact details as plain text near the top of the page body
- Common fonts like Arial, Calibri or Helvetica at 10 to 12 points
- Dates in one format, like "Mar 2023 – Present"
- Job titles and skills that match the wording of the job description, where true
- Saved as a text-based PDF or Word file

That doesn't mean it has to be dull. Bold job titles, clear spacing and a single accent colour are all fine.

## Keywords: the part people underrate

Formatting gets you read correctly. Keywords get you found. Recruiters search for job titles, tools, certifications and skills. If the job says "Power BI" and your resume says "dashboarding tools", you may not appear in a search for Power BI. Use the employer's words where they honestly describe your work. Our [keyword guide](/articles/resume-keywords-from-job-description/) shows how.

## ATS names you'll come across

You don't need to know which system an employer uses, but the names show up in application links and emails. Large companies often use Workday, SAP SuccessFactors, Oracle Recruiting (Taleo) or iCIMS. Fast-growing companies and startups often use Greenhouse, Lever, SmartRecruiters, Zoho Recruit or the recruiting module of their HR software. In India, job portals such as Naukri also give recruiters their own resume search tools.

They differ in detail, but they all depend on the same thing: reading your resume's text correctly. A resume that parses well in one usually parses well in the others.

## How recruiters search inside an ATS

A recruiter rarely reads every application. More often they:

- **Filter** on answers to knockout questions: location, notice period, work authorisation, minimum experience, sometimes expected salary.
- **Search** for job titles and must-have skills, often in combinations such as `"accounts executive" AND GST AND Tally`.
- **Sort** by match score, date applied or recent activity, and open the top results.

This is why the same resume can be "invisible" for one job and shortlisted for another. If your resume says "bookkeeping" and the recruiter searches for "accounts executive" and "GST", you may not surface, even if you've done the work.

## Where AI fits in

Newer systems add AI on top of the search: they summarise each candidate, compare the resume to the job description and suggest a ranking. AI matching is better at understanding related terms than old keyword search, but it still relies on clear structure and on your resume describing your work in the language of the job. Vague bullets give it little to work with. More in [how AI screens resumes](/articles/how-ai-screens-resumes/).

## Three things that are not true

- **"ATS rejects 75% of resumes automatically."** This figure circulates widely without a reliable source. Most systems store and rank; people decide.
- **"Hiding keywords in white text beats the ATS."** Parsers read hidden text as normal text, recruiters see it in the parsed view, and it looks like a trick.
- **"You need a special ATS template."** You need a plain, well-structured one. Any clean single-column layout works.

## How to test your resume

- Copy all the text from your PDF and paste it into a plain text editor. If it comes out jumbled or missing pieces, a parser will struggle too.
- Upload it to a job portal and check whether the auto-filled fields are right.
