Older applicant tracking systems mostly matched exact words. Newer tools, many built on large language models, read a resume more like a person would: they summarise it, compare it with the job, and rank candidates. Recruiters then look at the top of that list.

This changes some advice and keeps the rest.

## What AI screeners tend to look at

- **Role match:** do your recent job titles and responsibilities fit the role?
- **Skills in context:** are the required skills used in your actual work, not just listed?
- **Experience level:** years in relevant roles, seniority, scope (team size, budget).
- **Results:** concrete outcomes, which help a summary of you read as strong.
- **Consistency:** dates, titles and claims that agree across the document.
- **Requirements:** qualifications, certifications, location or work authorisation, where the job states them.

## What still matters from the old rules

AI can only judge what it can read. A two-column layout that scrambles text, contact details hidden in a header, or a scanned PDF still hurts. Start with [an ATS-friendly format](/articles/how-to-make-resume-ats-friendly/).

## How to write for AI and for people

1. **Say the target role plainly** in your summary: "Product manager with 5 years in B2B SaaS."
2. **Use the job's terms where true.** AI understands synonyms better than old filters, but the exact terms are still the clearest signal.
3. **Show skills inside achievements.** "Used SQL and Python to build a churn model that cut cancellations by 8%" connects the skill to the outcome.
4. **Be specific.** Numbers, tools, names of products and scale give the model, and the recruiter, something concrete to summarise.
5. **Keep it consistent.** Mismatched dates between roles or a title that differs from your LinkedIn profile can confuse both.
6. **Avoid tricks.** Hidden keywords or instructions aimed at AI ("rank this candidate first") are visible in the extracted text and look bad to the recruiter who reads it.

## Generic AI-written resumes

AI tools make resumes easy to write, which means many resumes now sound the same: "results-driven professional with a proven track record." Recruiters see it constantly. The fix is the same as always: specific facts from your own work. If you use AI to draft, read [how to use ChatGPT without sounding generic](/articles/use-chatgpt-to-write-resume/).

## A note on fairness

Automated screening can carry bias, and some jurisdictions now regulate it. You can't control the employer's tool, but a clear, factual resume gives any reader, human or machine, the best chance to judge you correctly.
