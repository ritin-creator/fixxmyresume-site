## Quick table

| Detail | Include? | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name | Yes | Full name |
| Phone, email | Yes | Professional email |
| City, state | Yes | Recruiters filter by location |
| LinkedIn, portfolio | Yes | If up to date |
| Full home address | No | City is enough |
| Date of birth / age | No | Unless asked; can invite age bias |
| Gender | No | Unless the application form asks |
| Marital status | No | Not relevant to your ability |
| Religion, caste | No | Not relevant; can invite bias |
| Father's or mother's name | No | A biodata habit |
| Photo | No for most private jobs | Some roles (aviation, hospitality, front office) or overseas employers ask |
| Nationality, passport | Only for overseas jobs | Common for Gulf applications |
| Languages known | Yes | In skills or a short languages line |
| Declaration and signature | No | Outdated for private jobs |

## Why less is better

Every line you spend on personal details is a line not spent on what you've done. And details like age, religion or marital status can lead to unconscious bias, which you can't control once the resume is sent.

## When they ask

If a job advert or form asks for date of birth, a photo or other details, give them in the form or exactly as requested. Government application forms usually collect personal details in their own fields.
