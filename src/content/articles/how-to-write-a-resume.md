A resume has one job: get you a conversation. Before a person reads it, software usually sorts it, and when a person does read it, they often skim the first page in under a minute. So a good resume is easy for both a machine and a hurried human to read.

This guide walks through it section by section.

## 1. Choose a layout that machines can read

Use a single column, standard section headings and a plain font. Most applicant tracking systems (ATS) pull text out of your file and drop it into fields. Two-column designs, tables, text boxes and icons can scramble that text or hide it. See [what an ATS resume is](/articles/what-is-an-ats-resume/) for the details.

The safe default is **reverse-chronological**: your most recent job first. It suits almost everyone except people with very little work history, who can lead with education and projects instead. The [format comparison](/articles/resume-formats-chronological-functional-hybrid/) covers when to use the others.

## 2. Put contact details at the top, in the body

Include:

- Full name
- Phone number
- Professional email address
- City (and country if you apply abroad)
- LinkedIn profile link, plus a portfolio or GitHub link if relevant

Keep these in the main body of the page, not in the document's header or footer area. Some systems skip headers and footers entirely.

## 3. Write a two-line summary aimed at the job

The summary is the first thing a recruiter reads. Name the role you want, your years of experience, and the one or two things you are best at.

> Sales manager with 6 years in FMCG distribution across North India. Grew a territory from 120 to 310 retail outlets and led a team of 9 field executives.

Skip generic lines like "hardworking professional seeking a challenging role". They tell the reader nothing. There are [30 summary examples here](/articles/resume-summary-examples/).

## 4. List experience as achievements, not duties

For each role, give the job title, company, city and dates, then three to six bullets. Each bullet should:

1. Start with an action verb (led, built, reduced, launched)
2. Say what you did
3. Show the result, with a number where you have one

Compare:

| Duty (weak) | Achievement (strong) |
| --- | --- |
| Responsible for handling customer complaints | Resolved 40+ customer complaints a week, cutting repeat complaints by a third |
| Worked on the company website | Rebuilt the product pages, cutting load time from 6 to 2 seconds |
| Managed social media | Grew Instagram followers from 2,000 to 11,000 in 8 months |

If you don't know the exact number, estimate honestly ("about 30 clients") or describe scale another way (team size, budget, frequency). Never invent figures. Our guide on [quantifying achievements](/articles/how-to-quantify-achievements-on-resume/) has more ways to find numbers.

## 5. Add education

List degree, institution, and year. Add your grade if it is strong or if the employer asks for it. Freshers can add relevant coursework, projects and academic awards here.

## 6. Add skills the job asks for

List hard skills (tools, languages, methods) that appear in the job description and that you can genuinely back up. Group them if the list is long:

- **Analytics:** SQL, Excel, Power BI
- **Languages:** English, Hindi, Marathi

Soft skills like "communication" carry more weight when shown in your bullets than when listed. See the [skills list](/articles/skills-to-put-on-a-resume/) for ideas.

## 7. Tailor it to each job

Read the job description and note the job title, the tools and the requirements it repeats. Make sure those words appear in your resume where they are true. This matters because recruiters search their ATS by keyword. Our [keyword guide](/articles/resume-keywords-from-job-description/) shows the 15-minute version.

## 8. Keep it the right length

One page if you have under about 8 years of experience; two pages if you have more. Cut anything that doesn't support the job you're applying for. More on [resume length](/articles/how-long-should-a-resume-be/).

## 9. Check it before you send it

Run through this list:

- [ ] File is a text-based PDF (you can select the text), unless Word is requested
- [ ] File name is your name, like `Priya-Sharma-Resume.pdf`
- [ ] No spelling mistakes in names of companies, tools or your own email
- [ ] Dates use one format throughout, like "Jan 2022 – Present"
- [ ] Every bullet starts with a verb, and most have a number
- [ ] The job title you want appears in the summary
- [ ] No "I", "me" or "my"

## What to leave out

- Photos, unless the country or employer expects them ([country guide](/articles/photo-on-resume-by-country/))
- "References available on request"
- Salary history, unless asked
- Unrelated hobbies that take up space

## One bullet, rewritten three times

Most resumes improve fastest in the bullets. Here's one line from an operations resume, rewritten step by step.

1. **Duty:** "Responsible for inventory management."
2. **Action:** "Managed inventory for a 12,000 sq ft warehouse."
3. **Action and result:** "Managed inventory for a 12,000 sq ft warehouse and cut stock mismatches from 3.2% to 0.8% with weekly cycle counts."

Each version is true, but only the third tells a recruiter what you achieved and how. If you don't have exact figures, use honest estimates ("about", "roughly") or other measures: time saved, volume handled, people trained, errors removed.

## What changes by career stage

| Stage | Length | Lead with | Keep short |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Student or fresher | 1 page | Education, projects, internships | School details beyond class X and XII |
| 1–5 years | 1 page | Experience with results | Education (one line each) |
| 5–15 years | 1–2 pages | Last 10 years of experience, key achievements | Early roles (title, company, dates) |
| 15+ years, leadership | 2 pages | Scope (teams, budgets, P&L), outcomes | Tools and junior-level detail |

## The short version

Clean single-column layout, a summary that names the role, achievement bullets with numbers, skills matched to the job, and a quick proofread. That combination handles both the software and the person reading after it.
